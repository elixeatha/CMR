Physics

MR physics in brief

Every image is a map of how quickly hydrogen protons in each tissue recover (T1) or lose (T2) their signal after a radiofrequency pulse. Sequences are recipes that make one of those differences dominate.

Net magnetisation

In the scanner field B0 (1.5 or 3 T), protons align to give a small net magnetisation along the bore. An RF pulse at the Larmor frequency (≈64 MHz at 1.5 T, 128 MHz at 3 T) tips it sideways, and the rotating transverse component induces the signal in the coils.

T1: longitudinal recovery

Time for magnetisation to regrow to 63% along B0. Fat recovers fast, blood slowly. Gadolinium shortens T1, so tissue with more contrast (scar, expanded extracellular space) is brighter on T1-weighted images.

T2: transverse decay

Time for the transverse signal to fall to 37% as spins dephase. Free water lengthens T2, so oedema is bright on T2-weighted images and raises T2 maps.

T2*: T2 plus field inhomogeneity

Always shorter than T2. Paramagnetic iron and susceptibility interfaces (lung, metal) speed up dephasing. That is why T2* measures myocardial iron.

TR and TE

Repetition time (TR) mainly controls T1 weighting; echo time (TE) controls T2/T2* weighting. Short TR + short TE = T1-weighted; long TR + long TE = T2-weighted.

Field strength

3 T gives more signal (SNR), so better perfusion and mapping resolution, but T1 values are longer, bSSFP banding is worse, and RF heating (SAR) and device issues are greater.

Relaxation curves for cardiac tissues

Approximate native values; real numbers depend on sequence and site. The contrast between curves at a chosen time point is the image contrast. Blood vs myocardium differ most in T2/T1 ratio, which is what bSSFP exploits.

Physics

k-space, intuitively

The scanner does not record pixels. It records spatial frequencies into a grid called k-space; a Fourier transform turns that grid into the image. Every k-space point contributes to every pixel.

  • Centre = coarse shapes and overall contrast. The moment the centre is acquired decides the image contrast (e.g. relative to the inversion pulse in LGE).
  • Periphery = edges and fine detail (spatial resolution).
  • k-space is filled line by line (phase-encode lines). More lines = more time. That is why a cine is assembled over several heartbeats and needs a breath-hold.
  • Inconsistency between lines (breathing, ectopics) produces ghosts copied along the phase-encode direction. Skipping lines to save time wraps the image onto itself, which parallel imaging (SENSE/GRAPPA) and compressed sensing undo using coil information.

Motion control

ECG gating and breath-holding

A heartbeat is far shorter than the time needed to fill k-space, so most CMR is segmented: each beat contributes a few lines for each cardiac phase, and the image assumes every beat is the same. Breathing is frozen with a breath-hold, usually at end-expiration because it is the most reproducible position between slices.

Segmented acquisition across heartbeats

Cine (top): retrospective gating acquires continuously and sorts lines into ~25–30 phases afterwards, covering the whole RR interval. LGE (bottom): one inversion pulse, a trigger delay, then a short readout in mid-diastolic diastasis, typically every other beat to let magnetisation recover.

Getting a good trigger

  • Flowing blood in B0 induces a voltage that inflates the T wave (magnetohydrodynamic effect). Vectorcardiographic (VCG) gating separates true R waves from it.
  • Poor ECG: reposition leads on the back, or use peripheral pulse gating (delayed by ~200 ms; acceptable for retrospective cine and flow).
  • The ECG cannot be used for ST-segment monitoring in the bore.

Arrhythmia and breathing

  • Ectopics or AF blur cine: use arrhythmia rejection, prospective triggering, or real-time (single-shot) cine at lower resolution.
  • Poor breath-holders: shorter breath-holds (fewer slices per hold, parallel imaging), single-shot or motion-corrected averaged LGE, navigator-gated free breathing.
  • Tachycardia: shorten the acquisition window; consider systolic acquisition for mapping and LGE.

    Tab 3 · Interpretation

    Reading a CMR study

    A fixed order stops you anchoring on the first abnormality. Function first, then tissue, then synthesis.

    On this tab
    Method

    A systematic approach

    1. Technique and image quality

      Field strength, contrast dose and timing, arrhythmia or breathing artefact, whether TI was optimised. Decide what you can and cannot conclude.

    2. Volumes and function

      LV and RV EDV, ESV, EF and LV mass, indexed to BSA, from the SAX stack. Regional wall motion by the 17-segment model. Compare with sex- and age-specific reference ranges.

    3. Morphology

      Wall thickness and pattern of hypertrophy, wall thinning, aneurysm, thrombus, trabeculation, atria, valves, pericardium (thickness, effusion), great vessels and extracardiac findings.

    4. Tissue characterisation

      Oedema (T2 map, T2-weighted STIR), then LGE: present or absent, pattern (subendocardial vs non-subendocardial), territory, transmurality, extent. Then mapping: native T1, T2, ECV, T2* as indicated.

    5. Perfusion and flow

      Stress perfusion defects matched against LGE (ischaemia vs infarct). Flow: stroke volumes, regurgitant fraction, Qp:Qs, peak velocities.

    6. Synthesis

      Ischaemic vs non-ischaemic; a unifying diagnosis or differential; prognostic markers (LGE extent, EF, RV function); answer the clinical question.

    Quantification

    Volumes and function

    CMR is the reference standard for ventricular volumes and EF because it measures a full 3D stack without geometric assumptions, with better interstudy reproducibility than 2D echo. Contour the SAX stack at end-diastole and end-systole; check the basal slice against long-axis views (atrium vs ventricle).

    Tissue

    LGE patterns

    The single most useful question: does the enhancement involve the subendocardium? Infarction starts at the subendocardium and spreads outwards (the wavefront phenomenon), so ischaemic LGE is subendocardial or transmural and follows a coronary territory. LGE that spares the subendocardium is non-ischaemic.

    Ischaemic heart disease

    Viability and quantitative perfusion

    In ischaemic LV dysfunction the questions are how much myocardium is scar, how much is alive but dysfunctional, and how much blood flow reaches it under stress.

    Hibernating myocardium

    Viable but chronically underperfused, with down-regulated contraction. Function may recover after revascularisation, over weeks to months.

    Stunned myocardium

    Viable, with perfusion restored (e.g. after primary PCI) but function still depressed. It recovers spontaneously over days to weeks.

    Scar

    Replacement fibrosis. It is bright on LGE and does not recover. Transmural extent sets the ceiling on recovery.

    Contractile reserve

    Low-dose dobutamine (5–10 µg/kg/min) cine shows whether a dysfunctional segment can thicken. It is most useful when LGE transmurality is intermediate (Wellnhofer 2004).

    LGE transmurality and likelihood of recovery

    Categories follow Kim et al. (NEJM 2000): the likelihood that a dysfunctional segment improves after revascularisation falls progressively with transmural extent, with 50% the usual dividing line. Choi et al. (2001) showed the same after acute infarction. A thinned segment with only a thin rim of scar can still recover, so wall thickness alone should not be used to call a segment non-viable.

    What the trials say. In STICH (Bonow 2011, SPECT/dobutamine echo) and REVIVED-BCIS2 (Perera 2022, PCI vs optimal medical therapy), viability testing did not identify patients who gained a survival benefit from revascularisation. CMR viability now informs, rather than decides, revascularisation. Scar burden on LGE remains a strong marker of prognosis and of arrhythmic risk.

    Quantitative perfusion: MBF and MPR

    Visual perfusion reading compares one territory with another, so it can miss disease that reduces flow everywhere (balanced three-vessel disease or microvascular dysfunction). Quantitative perfusion converts the first-pass signal into absolute myocardial blood flow (MBF, mL/g/min) for each pixel.

    • How: the arterial input function (blood-pool signal) saturates at high gadolinium concentration. A dual-bolus or dual-sequence method keeps it linear. The myocardial curve is then deconvolved with a tissue model. Inline pixel-wise MBF maps are now automatic on some scanners (Kellman 2017; Hsu 2018).
    • Myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) = stress MBF ÷ rest MBF. Rest MBF rises with the rate–pressure product, so tachycardia, hypertension, anaemia or anxiety lower MPR even when stress MBF is normal.
    • Reference values: in a two-centre healthy-volunteer study using inline mapping (Brown et al.), stress MBF averaged 2.24 ± 0.53 and rest MBF 0.62 ± 0.13 mL/g/min, with MPR 3.74 ± 1.00. Stress MBF was higher in women, and both stress MBF and MPR fell with age. Values also depend on method and field strength, so there is no single universal MPR cut-off.
    • Thresholds against invasive physiology (Kotecha 2019, 1.5 T adenosine). Regional stress MBF ≤ 1.94 mL/g/min detected FFR-positive (≤ 0.80) disease. Mean stress MBF was 1.47 ± 0.48 in FFR-positive regions, 2.10 ± 0.35 in microvascular-dysfunction regions (IMR ≥ 25) and 2.47 ± 0.50 in normal regions. In patients without a regional defect, global stress MBF < 1.82 separated three-vessel disease from microvascular dysfunction.
    • Prognosis: lower stress MBF and MPR were independently associated with death and MACE in over 1,000 patients (Knott 2020).

    Thresholds are from single studies with one method and field strength (Kotecha 2019; Brown et al.) and are shown for teaching. Always read MBF together with visual perfusion, LGE, the haemodynamic response to stress and the coronary anatomy.

    Tab 4 · Research techniques

    Beyond the clinical protocol

    Methods that measure metabolism, energetics and microstructure. Most are confined to specialist centres and research studies, but they explain a lot of the physiology behind what you see on routine scans.

    On this tab
    Energetics · Research, specialist centres

    Myocardial energetics and ³¹P spectroscopy

    The heart turns over its entire ATP pool every few seconds. Phosphocreatine (PCr) is the energy buffer: creatine kinase (CK) shuttles high-energy phosphate from PCr to ADP to keep ATP constant. When energy supply falls, PCr drops first, so the PCr/ATP ratio is a sensitive index of the heart's energetic reserve.

    What is measured

    Phosphorus-31 nuclei resonate at a different frequency from protons, so a dedicated ³¹P coil collects a spectrum. Each phosphorus compound appears as a peak at its own chemical shift (ppm). Peak areas give PCr/ATP; saturation-transfer methods add CK flux.

    Clinical findings

    PCr/ATP is reduced in heart failure of most causes, DCM, HCM (including genotype-positive, phenotype-negative carriers), aortic stenosis, diabetes and obesity. In DCM a reduced ratio predicted total and cardiovascular mortality (Neubauer 1997).

    Stress energetics

    Ischaemia during handgrip or dobutamine stress transiently lowers PCr/ATP in the affected myocardium. This gives a metabolic, not perfusion-based, marker of ischaemia.

    Limitations

    Low signal means large voxels (several mL, usually the septum), 10–30 min acquisitions, contamination from chest-wall muscle and blood, and method-dependent normal values. Field strength of 3 T or 7 T helps.

    Try it: a cardiac ³¹P spectrum

    Stylised spectrum (ppm axis runs right to left by convention; ATP multiplets drawn as single peaks). Blood contains ATP and 2,3-DPG but almost no PCr, so blood in the voxel inflates the ATP peaks and lowers the apparent ratio. The 2,3-DPG signal (which also hides inorganic phosphate) is used to correct for it.

    Metabolism · Research

    Proton (¹H) spectroscopy and myocardial lipid

    With the large water signal suppressed or used as a reference, a single-voxel proton spectrum in the septum shows triglyceride methylene at ~1.3 ppm, creatine at ~3.0 ppm and water at ~4.7 ppm. The lipid-to-water ratio quantifies intramyocardial triglyceride ("cardiac steatosis").

    • Myocardial triglyceride is raised in obesity, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes, before systolic dysfunction appears (McGavock 2007). It falls with weight loss and caloric restriction.
    • Lipotoxicity is one proposed link between metabolic disease and diastolic dysfunction / HFpEF.
    • Technique: PRESS or STEAM voxel (~6–8 mL) in the septum, ECG-triggered, with breath-holds or a respiratory navigator. Avoid epicardial fat, which contaminates the lipid peak.
    Metabolism · Early clinical research

    Hyperpolarised ¹³C metabolic imaging

    Dynamic nuclear polarisation boosts the MR signal of a 13C-labelled substrate, usually [1-13C]pyruvate, more than 10,000-fold. After intravenous injection, its conversion to downstream metabolites can be watched in real time, but only for about a minute before the polarisation decays.

    • Bicarbonate production reflects flux through pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), the gatekeeper of glucose oxidation.
    • Lactate and alanine reflect glycolytic exchange and the cytosolic redox state.
    • First human cardiac study in 2016 (Cunningham). In type 2 diabetes, cardiac PDH flux was reduced and rose after a glucose challenge (Rider 2020).
    • Needs an on-site polariser, a sterile fluid path and a timed injection; it is available at only a handful of centres.
    Microstructure · Research, specialist centres

    Diffusion tensor CMR (DT-CMR)

    Water diffuses more easily along cardiomyocytes than across them. Measuring diffusion in at least six directions gives a tensor in each voxel. Its main axis shows the local myocyte orientation, and its shape shows how organised the tissue is.

    Mean diffusivity (MD)
    Overall water mobility; rises with expanded extracellular space (fibrosis, oedema, infarction).
    Fractional anisotropy (FA)
    0 = random, 1 = fully directional. Falls with myocyte disarray or loss; low diastolic FA in HCM was linked to ventricular arrhythmia, independent of LGE and ECV (Ariga 2019).
    Helix angle (HA)
    Myocyte inclination relative to the short-axis plane. It rotates smoothly from about +60° at the endocardium to about −60° at the epicardium.
    Sheetlet angle (E2A)
    Orientation of the laminar "sheetlets" that slide and reorient to thicken the wall. Normally low in diastole and steeper in systole.

    Helix angle and sheetlets

    Disease signatures

    • HCM: low FA (disarray); sheetlets stay in a systolic-like configuration in diastole (impaired relaxation).
    • DCM: sheetlets fail to reorient in systole (reduced E2A mobility).
    • Infarction: high MD and low FA, with a loss of the normal helix-angle gradient.
    • The microstructural dynamics in HCM and DCM were described in vivo by Nielles-Vallespin (2017).

    Why it is hard

    • Diffusion displacements are microns; cardiac motion is millimetres. It needs motion-compensated spin echo (M2) or stimulated-echo (STEAM) sequences spanning two heartbeats.
    • Low SNR, so many breath-holds and averages and only a few slices.
    • Strong gradients (3 T research systems) and careful quality control of each image.
